## 2. Tasks, models, settings

- 652 task pairs, 12 repositories, 4 languages (Python, TypeScript, Go, Rust), 199 features (p.2; Table 3, p.21). The abstract says "over 600" (p.1). The earlier note is right.
- Each task: one repository state, two features, two sets of unit tests; each agent sees only its own feature (pp.3–4). 77.3% of tasks have conflicting ground-truth solutions (p.4).
- Pool count is inconsistent: 34 feature pools / PRs (p.4; Fig. 3 p.5; Table 3 total p.21); the #PRs column of Table 3 sums to 32, not 34 (p.21); and p.22 says "52 task sets".
- Feature size: mean 52.3 changed lines, 1.4 files; difficulty Easy 15 (8%), Medium 99 (50%), Hard 85 (43%) (Table 4, p.22).
- Five models: GPT-5, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, MiniMax-M2, Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 (pp.6–7). Agent framework: OpenHands v0.54 (p.6). Self-play only (same model on both sides) in the reported results.
- Settings: Solo (one agent implements both features) vs Coop (two agents, one feature each) (p.7); Coop with and without the communication tool (p.8).
- Scaling probe: 46 tasks from 3 task sets, 2 to 4 agents (p.8).
- No statement of repeated runs per task. Error bars are 95% Wilson intervals over tasks (p.7).

## 3. How budget is matched

It is matched on workload, not on resources.

- Solo is "one agent to implement both features" (p.3); the claim is framed as "a single agent given the same total workload" (p.2), "one agent for the same workload" (p.7).
- The only resource limit printed is a cap of 100 actions per agent (p.4), with a footnote that raising it gave no gains (p.4). The paper does not say whether Solo gets 100 or 200 actions.
- Tokens, cost and wall-clock time are not reported for either arm anywhere in the PDF.

Consequence for us: in Coop two agents each have up to 100 actions, so the collective plausibly has at least as much budget as Solo, which makes the Coop deficit conservative. But it is not a token-matched comparison and cannot be described as one. Our standard (same total tokens) is stricter and measures something this paper leaves open.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-5 | 0.48 | 0.28 | 315 | 183 | 42% | 0.64 |
| Claude Sonnet 4.5 | 0.47 | 0.26 | 307 | 168 | 45% | 0.60 |
| MiniMax-M2 | 0.36 | 0.14 | 236 | 91 | 61% | 0.46 |
| Qwen3-Coder | 0.22 | 0.13 | 141 | 87 | 38% | 0.63 |
| Qwen3-Instruct | 0.06 | 0.05 | 41 | 30 | 27% | 0.68 |
| Pooled | — | — | 1039 | 558 | 46% | 0.59 |

- Pooled AUC: Solo 0.338, Coop 0.200, gap 0.138 (Table 5, p.25). "On average, 41% of Solo capability is lost" (p.24).
- The gap is largest at mid difficulty and closes for very easy and very hard tasks (Fig. 4 right, p.7; Fig. 7, p.24).
- More agents: 68.6% with 2, 46.5% with 3, 30.0% with 4, on 46 tasks (p.8). This compares team sizes with each other, not against Solo.
- Coop never beats Solo for any model (Fig. 4 left, p.7).

Headline numbers versus the tables:

- "30% lower success rates" on average (abstract, p.1). Printed, but I cannot reproduce it. Pooled counts give a 46% relative drop, mean of per-model relative drops is about 43%, the AUC measure gives 41% (p.24), and the mean absolute gap is about 15 points. The paper does not say how 30% was computed.
- "only 25%" for GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5, "around 50% lower" than Solo (p.3; "roughly half", p.15). The figure and table give 28% and 26%, which are 42% and 45% below Solo (Fig. 4 p.7; Table 5 p.25). The text rounds in the direction of the claim. Section 4 says the gap is "as large as 50%" for the three leading models (p.7); only MiniMax (61%) reaches 50% on the counts.
- Minor: Table 5 counts (183, 168) and Table 6 with-comm final rates (27.90%, 25.92%, i.e. about 182 and 169 of 652) differ by one task each (p.25, p.27).

## 5. Decentralised / peer-to-peer coordination

The whole Coop setting is peer-to-peer: two equal agents, no orchestrator, no imposed workflow, asynchronous free-form messages (p.4, p.6). The paper positions this against scaffolded or orchestrated systems, which it says "mask rather than solve" the coordination problem (p.14). Its finding is that unscaffolded peer messaging under workspace isolation fails. Rare successful runs show role division, resource division (line-level ownership) and negotiation (pp.13–14). The authors say these are "rare in our traces" (p.14).

## 7. CooperBench specifics

Communication channel:
- A message-sending tool built on an SQL database; a sent message is included in the recipient's next-step prompt (p.6). Open-ended natural language (p.4). Exposed to the agent as an MCP tool `openhands_comm_send` (p.26).
- It is "the only channel agents could use to coordinate" (p.8). Each agent works in its own container and cannot see the other's branch (p.6, p.11).
- The Coop prompt is already tuned for coordination: it tells agents to share line ranges early, forbids insertion markers, and requires a final status message (p.26). The failures occur with this prompt.

Effect of communication (with vs without the tool):
- No significant difference in success for any model (Fig. 5a, p.8). Final success after all merge steps, no-comm vs with-comm: GPT-5 27.91 vs 27.90; Claude 27.30 vs 25.92; MiniMax 14.88 vs 13.96; Qwen3-Coder 14.72 vs 13.34; Qwen3-Instruct 3.37 vs 4.60; average 17.64 vs 17.14, Δ −0.5 (Table 6, p.27).
- Communication lowers the naive merge-conflict rate for Claude, GPT-5, MiniMax and Qwen Instruct (p.9). Approximate conflict rates read from Fig. 5(b) (p.8), with comm vs no comm: Claude about 20% vs 42%; GPT-5 about 27% vs 66%; MiniMax about 16% vs 28%; Qwen about 42% vs 52%; Qwen Coder about 70% vs 71%. The caption says "across all models" (p.8); Qwen Coder shows no visible reduction and the text omits it (p.9).
- Communication share of all execution events: Claude 20.0%, GPT-5 16.3%, MiniMax 13.6%, Qwen 6.2%, Qwen Coder 3.3% (Fig. 5c, p.8). Text: "as much as 20% of the steps" (p.9).
- A plan message in the first turn goes with a conflict rate of 29.4% vs 51.5% (p.9). Conflict-free trajectories have a Plan:Question ratio of 2.04 vs 1.31 (p.9). These are correlations.
- Communication errors per conversation, largest values: repetition 37.1% (Claude), unresponsiveness 21.3% (MiniMax), hallucination 6.9% (Claude) (Fig. 6, p.10).

Merge-conflict share of failures:
- Not reported. The paper gives conflict rates as a percentage of tasks (Fig. 5b, p.8) and success after each merge strategy (Table 6, p.27), never the fraction of failed Coop runs that failed at the merge step.
- Related: with communication, average success moves from 10.71% (naive merge) to 15.97% (union) to 17.14% (learned resolver) (Table 6, p.27). So textual conflicts that an automatic resolver can fix account for about 6.4 points of final success; the rest of the gap to Solo is not textual.
- The paper's reading: conflicts are a "spatial" problem that messaging helps; success needs "semantic" agreement that messaging did not deliver (p.9; case study pp.31–33).

Failure taxonomy:
- Causes, from manual review of 50 failed Coop traces: Expectation 42%, Commitment 32%, Communication 26% (Table 2, p.12; method p.10). The earlier note is right. With n = 50 each share carries roughly ±13 points of sampling error (my estimate, not the paper's).
- Symptoms, labelled by GPT-5 as judge over all failed Coop trajectories: work overlap 33.2%, divergent architecture 29.7%, repetition 14.7%, unresponsiveness 8.7%, unverifiable claims 4.3%, broken commitment 3.7%, dependency access 1.7%, placeholder misuse 1.5%, parameter flow 1.3%, timing dependency 1.1% (Table 1, p.11). Judge agreed with humans on 48 of 50 cases (p.29).

Shared-state mechanisms:
- None was tested. No shared git branch, no shared task list, no scratchpad, no visibility of the partner's diff. Agents are deliberately isolated and restricted to natural-language messages (p.15).
- The paper names the gap: collaboration likely needs mechanisms that turn conversation into "verifiable shared state" (p.13), and it lists "lightweight protocols for verifiable commitments" and richer channels such as screen sharing as future work (p.6, p.16).
- It notes that other benchmarks "enforce turn-taking or shared observability" and that CooperBench does not (p.15).
- The paper says it does not compare frameworks or "creative methods to enhance coordination" and invites others to do so (p.6).

## 8. Stated limitations

There is no limitations section. Scattered statements: computer-use tools limited to local file and terminal operations (p.4); no comparison of agent frameworks or coordination methods (p.6); the 2-to-4-agent result is a "small scale experiment" (p.8); emergent coordination behaviours are rare (p.14). The conclusion asserts that findings "generalize to any domain" with role and resource conflicts (p.16); nothing in the paper tests that.

Unstated weaknesses I see: no token or cost accounting; apparently one run per task; self-play only; one agent framework; cause shares from 50 hand-labelled traces; the headline 30% and 25%/50% figures do not match the tables; Solo's action cap is unspecified.

## 10. What our introduction can safely write

Safe, from the tables: "On CooperBench, 652 paired coding tasks from 12 open-source libraries, two agents that coordinate only by messaging succeed less often than one agent given both features, for all five models tested: 28% against 48% for GPT-5 and 26% against 47% for Claude Sonnet 4.5" (p.2; Fig. 4 p.7; Table 5 p.25). Safe: "pooled over models, about 41% of solo capability is lost" (p.24, Table 5 p.25). Safe: "giving the agents a message channel did not raise success (17.1% with it, 17.6% without), although it reduced merge conflicts, and in the heaviest case took 20% of all execution steps" (Table 6 p.27; Fig. 5 p.8; p.9). Safe and useful to us: "the agents worked in isolated workspaces with messaging as the only channel; no shared-state mechanism was evaluated, and the authors point to verifiable shared state as a direction" (p.8, p.13, p.15). If quoting the paper's own headline, attribute it: the authors report "30% lower success rates" on average (p.1) and "only 25%" for the two leading models (p.3); both are looser than their tables. Do not write that the comparison is budget-matched in tokens, and do not write "20% of the budget": the unit is execution events, and 20.0% is the maximum, for Claude only (Fig. 5c, p.8).
